## What are the strongest arguments on each side?

### Takeaway
The "pro-label" case rests on (a) a functional/processual definition of fascism (Paxton's "stages," Stanley's "tactics," Eco-style traits) plus (b) concrete behavior: incitement of political violence on Jan 6, rhetoric of national rebirth and dehumanization, and, in the second term, state repression of dissenters. The "anti-label" case rests on (a) generic/ideal-type definitions (Griffin, Payne, Evans) requiring a revolutionary, palingenetic, totalitarian project, mass paramilitary party, and expansionist war, and (b) a political/strategic argument (Moyn, Robin, Riley) that the analogy exoticizes a very American phenomenon, defends a failing status quo, and misreads a weak, incoherent, patrimonial regime. Many empirical political scientists sidestep the label in favor of "competitive authoritarianism" or "patrimonialism."

### Inferences
- The disagreement is largely definitional: process/behavior-based definitions (Paxton, Stanley) yield "yes"; ideal-type/ideology-based definitions (Griffin, Payne) yield "no." The left skeptics (Moyn, Robin, Riley) mostly argued from political consequences and regime weakness, which made their position more vulnerable once the second term showed state capacity for repression.
- Evans's "consensus of specialists" claim (2021) is dated; by 2025 several listed and unlisted scholars had moved toward the label.

## How have scholars' positions shifted over time, particularly after January 6 and during the 2025–2026 second term?

### Takeaway
Two waves of conversion: Jan 6, 2021 (Paxton most prominently) and spring 2025 (state repression of dissent — e.g., the Khalil arrest — university coercion). Several prominent skeptics (Moyn, Robin) partly reversed in 2025; others (Evans/Payne/Griffin) have not been shown to have changed. Empirical democracy scholars shifted from "backsliding" to "competitive authoritarianism" by spring 2025.

### Cited Findings
- Paxton: resisted the label 2016–2020, switched Jan 11, 2021 after Jan 6, reaffirmed in Oct 2024 NYT interview. — [Columbia History](https://history.columbia.edu/2024/10/24/robert-paxton-was-interviewed-by-the-new-york-times-on-trumps-relation-to-fascism-and-how-january-6th-inspired-a-reconsideration-of-whether-or-not-trump-could-be-considered-a-fascist/)

## What do quantitative democracy indices and polls say?

### Takeaway
Every major index registered a sharp 2025 decline: V-Dem downgraded the U.S. from liberal to electoral democracy (rank 20th → 51st); Freedom House hit its lowest U.S. score (81/100); EIU its lowest (7.65, "flawed democracy"); Century Foundation's new meter fell 79 → 57 ("collapsing"); Bright Line Watch experts fell from 67 to a low of 53, partially rebounding to 60 by July 2026. None classifies the U.S. as fascist or closed autocracy; the indices describe erosion toward hybrid/competitive authoritarianism with elections still functioning.

## Alternative framings and key structural divergences from historical fascism

### Takeaway
The leading alternatives are competitive authoritarianism (Levitsky & Way), patrimonialism (Hanson & Kopstein; Riley's "ramshackle patrimonialism and weak charisma"), and radical-right populism (Griffin, Mudde). Divergences scholars cite: no mass paramilitary party, no coherent revolutionary/totalitarian ideology, continuing elections/courts/federalism, operating (if pressured) press, transactional personalism, no expansionist war aim (Evans; Payne cited noninterventionism), and no interwar context of mass mobilization against a revolutionary left (Riley).

### Cited Findings
- Competitive authoritarianism: elections continue but state institutions are weaponized against opponents; outward appearance of constitutional democracy while institutions are "hollowed out." — [Fair Observer](https://www.fairobserver.com/politics/america-crosses-into-competitive-authoritarian-rule-under-trump/); [Foreign Affairs](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/node/1132777)
- Patrimonialism (Stephen E. Hanson & Jeffrey S. Kopstein, *The Assault on the State*, 2024): ruler governs the state "as if it were his personal possession," like a family business, distributing parts of the state to loyalists, cronies and family, attacking impersonal administration as an obstacle; future where to get a road fixed "we have to kiss the ring of a Don Corleone or a Donald Trump."
