# Scholarly Frameworks for Defining and Identifying Fascism and Authoritarianism

Scope note: these notes cover the frameworks themselves. Authors' own applications to US politics are mentioned only in passing. Where wording comes from my background knowledge and I could not verify it against a fetched source during this session, it is flagged **[unverified]** and also listed under Gaps.

## Q0 (Objective): What does each framework define, and what are its criteria/indicators?

### Takeaway
The frameworks fall into two families. **Fascism frameworks** (Paxton, Eco, Griffin, Payne, Stanley, Finchelstein, Britt) define a specific, historically rooted phenomenon: ultranationalist, mobilizational, violent, anti-liberal and focused on national rebirth. **Authoritarianism/backsliding frameworks** (Linz, Levitsky & Ziblatt, Levitsky & Way, Bermeo, Snyder, Ben-Ghiat) describe how leaders take apart democratic constraints, and most of their criteria are observable behaviors. The most checklist-ready tool is Levitsky & Ziblatt's four-indicator "litmus test" with its 15 sub-questions (2018, pp. 18-19).

### Cited Findings

#### 1. Robert O. Paxton, *The Anatomy of Fascism* (2004)
- **Definition (verbatim):** fascism is "a form of political behavior marked by obsessive preoccupation with community decline, humiliation, or victimhood and by compensatory cults of unity, energy, and purity, in which a mass-based party of committed nationalist militants, working in uneasy but effective collaboration with traditional elites, abandons democratic liberties and pursues with redemptive violence and without legal or ethical restraints goals of internal cleansing and external expansion." — [enotes / search summary of Paxton](https://www.enotes.com/topics/anatomy-fascism); [Wikipedia, Definitions of fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism). (The page number is usually given as p. 218 of the Knopf edition **[unverified]**.)
- **The nine "mobilizing passions" (verbatim where quoted):**
  1. "A sense of overwhelming crisis beyond the reach of any traditional solutions"
  2. "The primacy of the group, toward which one has duties superior to every right, whether universal or individual, and the subordination of the individual to it"
  3. "The belief that one's group is a victim, a sentiment that justifies any action, without legal or moral limits, against its enemies"
  4. "Dread of the group's decline under the corrosive effects of individualistic liberalism, class conflict, and alien influences"
  5. "The need for closer integration of a purer community, by consent if possible, or by exclusionary violence if necessary"
  6. "The need for authority by natural chiefs (always male), culminating in a national chieftain who alone is capable of incarnating the group's destiny"
  7. "The superiority of the leader's instincts over abstract and universal reason"
  8. "The beauty of violence and the efficacy of will, when they are devoted to the group's success"
  9. "The right of the chosen people to dominate others without restraint from any kind of human or divine law…" (Wikipedia confirms the ninth passion as "right of chosen people"; the full wording, "…right being decided by the sole criterion of the group's prowess within a Darwinian struggle", is **[unverified]**.)
  — Items 1-8 are quoted at [Gardner, "Is It Fascism?"](https://dgardner.substack.com/p/is-it-fascism); the list and the ninth item are at [Wikipedia, Definitions of fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism). The longer clauses in items 2-4 and 6 are from my memory of the book **[partly unverified]**. The passions appear in the book's concluding chapter, "What Is Fascism?" (pp. 219-220 commonly cited **[unverified]**).
- **Five stages:** (1) the creation of the movements; (2) their rooting in the political system; (3) their seizure of power; (4) their exercise of power; (5) the long duration, in which the regime chooses either radicalization or entropy. — [Riley, "Enigmas of Fascism," New Left Review II/30](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii30/articles/dylan-riley-enigmas-of-fascism); [search summary](https://www.nakedcapitalism.com/2019/07/robert-o-paxtons-the-five-stages-of-fascism.html). The stage model was first set out in Paxton, "The Five Stages of Fascism," *Journal of Modern History* 70:1 (1998).
  - Stage 2 is "rooting in the political system through alliance with elites who believe they can use it." Stage 3 is conservatives "seeking to control rising leftist opposition invite fascists to share power." In Stage 5 the regime either radicalizes (Nazi Germany) or "slips into traditional authoritarian rule" (Fascist Italy). — [search summary of Paxton stages](https://www.nakedcapitalism.com/2019/07/robert-o-paxtons-the-five-stages-of-fascism.html)
- **Method:** Paxton defines fascism by what fascists *did* (political behavior, function and trajectory), not by ideology. He sees fascism as "counter-revolutionary" and "opportunistic". Michael Mann, by contrast, holds that fascists had "a powerful ideology, a coherent social base and a distinctive set of goals." — [Riley, NLR](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii30/articles/dylan-riley-enigmas-of-fascism)
- **Preconditions Paxton identifies:** the experience of WWI, the success of the Bolshevik Revolution, mass socialist parties, and cultural themes that elevate "race, community or nation over those of the individual and of reason." — [Riley, NLR](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii30/articles/dylan-riley-enigmas-of-fascism)
- **Limitations:** The stage model is built from two cases, Italy and Germany. Riley presents Paxton's behavioral, opportunist reading as one side of a live debate with ideology-centered scholars such as Mann. — [Riley, NLR](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii30/articles/dylan-riley-enigmas-of-fascism)

#### 2. Umberto Eco, "Ur-Fascism," *New York Review of Books*, 22 June 1995
- The 14 features, with key phrases verbatim: (1) **cult of tradition**; (2) **rejection of modernism**: "Ur-Fascism can be defined as irrationalism"; (3) **action for action's sake**: "Action being beautiful in itself, it must be taken before, or without, any previous reflection"; (4) **disagreement is treason**: "disagreement is a sign of modernism … disagreement is treason"; (5) **fear of difference**: it "seeks for consensus by exploiting and exacerbating the natural fear of difference"; (6) **appeal to a frustrated middle class**; (7) **obsession with a plot**: "at the root of the Ur-Fascist psychology there is the obsession with a plot"; (8) **enemies are both strong and weak**: "the enemies are at the same time too strong and too weak"; (9) **life is permanent warfare**: "pacifism is trafficking with the enemy"; (10) **popular elitism (contempt for the weak)**: "Every citizen belongs to the best people of the world"; (11) **cult of heroism and death**; (12) **machismo**: "disdain for women and intolerance and condemnation of nonstandard sexual habits"; (13) **selective (qualitative) populism**: "individuals as individuals have no rights … the People is conceived as a quality", which Eco links to TV/Internet populism; (14) **Newspeak**: "an impoverished vocabulary, and an elementary syntax, in order to limit the instruments for complex and critical reasoning." — [Eco, NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/)
- **Threshold claim:** the features "cannot be organized into a system; many of them contradict each other," yet "it is enough that one of them be present to allow fascism to coagulate around it." Eco calls fascism a "fuzzy" totalitarianism. — [Eco, NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism)
- **Limitations:** This is an essay drawing on Eco's personal and literary experience, not a scholarly typology. The "one feature is enough" threshold makes the list prone to over-inclusion, a version of the concept-stretching concern that critics raise about the popular use of "fascism" generally. — [Eco, NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/); [San Diego Jewish World review of Stanley (on overuse)](https://www.sdjewishworld.com/?p=114480)

#### 3. Roger Griffin, *The Nature of Fascism* (1991)
- **Definition:** fascism is "a political ideology whose mythic core in its various permutations is a palingenetic form of populist ultra-nationalism." — [Wikipedia, Definitions of fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism)
- **Fascist minimum:** the synthesis of palingenesis (national rebirth after a period of decadence), populism and ultranationalism. Griffin argues this synthesis "differentiates fascism from para-fascism and other authoritarian, nationalist ideologies," and without it there is no "true fascism." — [Wikipedia, Palingenetic ultranationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism)
- Griffin describes fascism as "a revolutionary form of nationalism" and part of a "new consensus" defining it as a genuinely revolutionary, trans-class, anti-liberal nationalism. — [Wikipedia, Definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism); [Library of Social Science excerpt](https://www.libraryofsocialscience.com/ideologies/resources/griffin-the-palingenetic-core/)
- **Critique:** Dave Renton and other Marxist scholars object to the "new consensus" for its focus on fascists' self-image and ideas. They argue scholars should study fascist activism and violence instead of searching for a "fascist minimum." — [Wikipedia, Palingenetic ultranationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism)
- **Use as a checklist:** the test is ideological. Is there a myth of national rebirth from decadence, ultranationalist and populist, that seeks a *revolutionary* new order? Regimes that use fascist trappings but are conservative or authoritarian in substance count as "para-fascist." — [Wikipedia, Palingenetic ultranationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism)

#### 4. Stanley G. Payne, typological description (*A History of Fascism, 1914-1945*, 1995; earlier *Fascism: Comparison and Definition*, 1980)
- Three groups of criteria:
  - **A. Ideology and goals:** nationalist authoritarianism (a new state); a regulated, multi-class, integrated national economy; a positive view of violence and war; the goal of empire/expansion.
  - **B. Fascist negations:** anti-liberalism, anti-communism, anti-conservatism (though willing to ally with the right).
  - **C. Style and organization:** mass mobilization and militarization of political relations; emphasis on aesthetic structure, symbolism and political liturgy; exaltation of the masculine principle; valorization of youth; an "authoritarian, charismatic, personal style of command."
  — [Wikipedia, Definitions of fascism (citing Payne 1995)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism)
- **Distinguishing feature:** Payne's "anti-conservatism" negation separates fascists from the authoritarian right (in his related scheme, the "radical right" and "conservative authoritarian right"), whom fascists might ally with but did not share goals with **[the sub-typology names are unverified]**. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism)
- **Limitations:** Payne himself presents the list as a descriptive typology, not a strict definition, and it is anchored in interwar European cases **[unverified characterization; no critique source fetched]**.

#### 5. Jason Stanley, *How Fascism Works: The Politics of Us and Them* (2018)
- **The ten pillars (one chapter each):** (1) the mythic past; (2) propaganda; (3) anti-intellectualism; (4) unreality; (5) hierarchy; (6) victimhood; (7) law and order; (8) sexual anxiety; (9) Sodom and Gomorrah (favoring "the heartland" over corrupt cities); (10) *Arbeit macht frei* (dismantling public goods and unions). — [publisher/retailer description via search](https://shop.arizona.edu/9780525511854-TRADE); [Libby](https://share.libbyapp.com/title/3758632)
- **Framing:** Stanley treats fascism as a set of *tactics* (fascist politics) that divide people into "us" and "them," which can exist without a fascist regime. He draws examples from Hungary, Poland, India, Myanmar and the US. — [Libby/publisher description](https://share.libbyapp.com/title/3758632)
- **Critiques:** reviewers say the analysis of concepts is thin, the examples are selective and alternative explanations are not engaged. Some find "fascism" used too broadly, which risks trivialization ("inure ourselves to actual fascism"). One review likens the book to "Ikea assembly instructions": a parts list without a causal account of how the whole works. — [San Diego Jewish World review](https://www.sdjewishworld.com/?p=114480); [Mises Wire](https://mises.org/wire/you-wont-learn-much-about-fascism-book-fascism); [Cleveland Review of Books](https://clereviewofbooks.com/2018-09-13-2018-9-13-howfascismworks/). (The Mises Institute is ideologically libertarian. Treat it as a partisan critique.)

#### 6. Timothy Snyder, *On Tyranny: Twenty Lessons from the Twentieth Century* (2017)
- **The twenty lessons (verbatim titles):** 1 Do not obey in advance; 2 Defend institutions; 3 Beware the one-party state; 4 Take responsibility for the face of the world; 5 Remember professional ethics; 6 Be wary of paramilitaries; 7 Be reflective if you must be armed; 8 Stand out; 9 Be kind to our language; 10 Believe in truth; 11 Investigate; 12 Make eye contact and small talk; 13 Practice corporeal politics; 14 Establish a private life; 15 Contribute to good causes; 16 Learn from peers in other countries; 17 Listen for dangerous words; 18 Be calm when the unthinkable arrives; 19 Be a patriot; 20 Be as courageous as you can. — [Snyder's Substack, "On Tyranny"](https://snyder.substack.com/p/on-tyranny)
- **Warning signs implied by the lessons:** citizens and institutions complying in advance ("most of the power of authoritarianism is freely given"); one-party consolidation; paramilitaries ("men with guns … start wearing uniforms"); the abandonment of facts ("To abandon facts is to abandon freedom. If nothing is true, then no one can criticize power"); use of emergencies or terror attacks to end checks and balances ("the oldest trick in the Hitlerian book"); dangerous words such as "emergency" and "exception"; and the need for complicit professionals (lawyers, judges, civil servants). — [search summary of lessons](https://new-ideal.aynrand.org/p/crisis-lies-morality-snyders-lessons-on-tyranny); [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/amp/1951921)
- **Limitations:** this is a prescriptive civic pamphlet, "a list of twenty punchy … lessons from history, rather than a sustained argument." It has no formal definition of tyranny or fascism. — [New Ideal review](https://new-ideal.aynrand.org/p/crisis-lies-morality-snyders-lessons-on-tyranny)

#### 7. Steven Levitsky & Daniel Ziblatt, *How Democracies Die* (2018) and *Tyranny of the Minority* (2023)
- **Four key indicators of authoritarian behavior** (Table 1, adapted from pp. 18-19; sub-questions verbatim):
  1. **Rejection of (or weak commitment to) democratic rules of the game.** (a) Do they reject the Constitution or express a willingness to violate it? (b) Do they suggest a need for antidemocratic measures, such as canceling elections, violating or suspending the Constitution, banning certain organizations, or restricting basic civil or political rights? (c) Do they seek to use (or endorse the use of) extraconstitutional means to change the government, such as military coups, violent insurrections, or mass protests aimed at forcing a change in the government? (d) Do they attempt to undermine the legitimacy of elections, for example, by refusing to accept credible electoral results?
  2. **Denial of the legitimacy of political opponents.** (a) Do they describe their rivals as subversive, or opposed to the existing constitutional order? (b) Do they claim that their rivals constitute an existential threat, either to national security or to the prevailing way of life? (c) Do they baselessly describe their partisan rivals as criminals, whose supposed violation of the law (or potential to do so) disqualifies them from full participation in the political arena? (d) Do they baselessly suggest that their rivals are foreign agents, in that they are secretly working in alliance with (or the employ of) a foreign government, usually an enemy one?
  3. **Toleration or encouragement of violence.** (a) Do they have any ties to armed gangs, paramilitary forces, militias, guerrillas, or other organizations that engage in illicit violence? (b) Have they or their partisan allies sponsored or encouraged mob attacks on opponents? (c) Have they tacitly endorsed violence by their supporters by refusing to unambiguously condemn it and punish it? (d) Have they praised (or refused to condemn) other significant acts of political violence, either in the past or elsewhere in the world?
  4. **Readiness to curtail civil liberties of opponents, including media.** (a) Have they supported laws or policies that restrict civil liberties, such as expanded libel or defamation laws or laws restricting protest, criticism of the government, or certain civic or political organizations? (b) Have they threatened to take legal or other punitive action against critics in rival parties, civil society, or the media? (c) Have they praised repressive measures taken by other governments, either in the past or elsewhere in the world?
  — [Table 1, adapted from Levitsky & Ziblatt 2018, pp. 18-19 (UMCS journal supplementary file)](https://journals.umcs.pl/al/article/downloadSuppFile/12210/6186); [Independent Review](https://www.independent.org/tir/2018-fall/how-democracies-die/)
  - The authors say a politician who meets even one criterion is cause for concern **[unverified wording]**. The test was adapted from Juan Linz's *The Breakdown of Democratic Regimes* (1978) **[unverified in fetched sources, but widely stated]**.
- **Soft guardrails (unwritten norms):**
  - **Mutual toleration:** rivals have an equal right to exist, compete for power and govern as long as they play by constitutional rules. The opposition, even if "misguided," is legitimate.
  - **Institutional forbearance:** avoiding acts that, "while respecting the letter of the law, obviously violate its spirit." Restraint in using one's legal prerogatives so as not to cripple the opposition.
  — [Webster University human rights blog review](https://blogs.webster.edu/humanrights/2021/05/07/book-review-how-democracies-die/); [Christian Century](https://christiancentury.org/print/pdf/node/34422)
  - They call these "the indispensable guardrails of stable democracy." The opposite of forbearance is "constitutional hardball" (a term from Mark Tushnet) **[unverified in fetched source]**. — [Webster blog](https://blogs.webster.edu/humanrights/2021/05/07/book-review-how-democracies-die/)
- ***Tyranny of the Minority* (2023):** introduces Linz's **"semi-loyal democrats"**: politicians who nominally support democracy but tolerate, excuse or protect co-partisans' extralegal and violent efforts to subvert it. The book's argument is that "when mainstream politicians tolerate, condone, or protect antidemocratic extremists … democracies get into trouble." — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/09/scholars-warn-of-danger-in-an-outdated-constitution-democracy-tyranny-of-the-minority); [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/tyranny-of-the-minority-writers-say-constitution-not-strong-enough-to-protect-democracy)
  - It also points to **counter-majoritarian institutions** (Senate, Electoral College, Article V, filibuster, judicial review) that let partisan minorities entrench themselves, and it proposes structural reforms. — [American Prospect review (Aziz Huq)](https://prospect.org/2023/12/01/2023-12-01-democracys-deserters-levitsky-ziblatt-review/)
- **Critiques:** Huq faults *Tyranny of the Minority* for neglecting economic drivers ("neoliberal turn," corporate power) and for reforms that face "practically insurmountable political odds." — [American Prospect](https://prospect.org/2023/12/01/2023-12-01-democracys-deserters-levitsky-ziblatt-review/). A common critique of *How Democracies Die*, that its focus on norms romanticizes a past "norms era" built on racial exclusion, has not been verified from a fetched source **[gap]**.

#### 8. Juan J. Linz, totalitarian vs. authoritarian regimes (1964; *Totalitarian and Authoritarian Regimes*, 1975/2000)
- **Authoritarian regime (verbatim):** "Political systems with limited, not responsible, political pluralism, without elaborate and guiding ideology, but with distinctive mentalities, without extensive nor intensive political mobilization, except at some points in their development, and in which a leader or occasionally a small group exercises power within formally ill-defined limits but actually quite predictable ones." — [Populism Studies glossary](https://www.populismstudies.org/Vocabulary/authoritarianism/); [SAGE Encyclopedia of Political Science](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/intlpoliticalscience/chpt/authoritarian-regimes)
- Four dimensions: (1) limited pluralism, between democracy's near-unlimited pluralism and totalitarianism's near-absence; (2) *mentality*, "less systematic and more amorphous … more emotional," versus *ideology*; (3) low or absent mobilization; (4) ill-defined but predictable limits on the leader. — [Populism Studies](https://www.populismstudies.org/Vocabulary/authoritarianism/)
- **Totalitarian regime (Linz's contrast, from memory [unverified]):** a monistic (not necessarily monolithic) center of power; an exclusive, elaborate ideology; and active, extensive mass mobilization through a single party and its organizations.
- **Relevance:** in Linz's terms fascist *movements* aim at totalitarian-style mobilization and ideology. Many right-wing dictatorships (e.g., Franco's Spain, the case Linz built his concept on) were "authoritarian," not totalitarian. Linz's *Breakdown of Democratic Regimes* (1978) also supplies the loyal / semi-loyal / disloyal opposition typology later used by Levitsky & Ziblatt. — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/09/scholars-warn-of-danger-in-an-outdated-constitution-democracy-tyranny-of-the-minority)

#### 9. Competitive authoritarianism (Levitsky & Way) and democratic backsliding / executive aggrandizement (Bermeo)
- **Competitive authoritarianism (verbatim):** "In competitive authoritarian regimes, formal democratic institutions are widely viewed as the principal means of obtaining and exercising political authority. Incumbents violate those rules so often and to such an extent, however, that the regime fails to meet conventional minimum standards for democracy." — Levitsky & Way, "Elections Without Democracy: The Rise of Competitive Authoritarianism," *Journal of Democracy* 13:2 (April 2002) — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/17196/summary); [NED / JoD classics](https://ned.org/journal-of-democracy-classics-the-rise-of-competitive-authoritarianism-levitsky-way)
  - Their minimum democratic criteria are free and fair elections, broad adult suffrage, protection of civil liberties (press, association), and the absence of non-elected tutelary authorities. They name four arenas of contestation: elections, legislature, judiciary and media. A later formulation describes the playing field as "uneven" (*Competitive Authoritarianism*, 2010) **[unverified in fetched sources]**.
- **Bermeo, "On Democratic Backsliding," *Journal of Democracy* 27:1 (2016):** backsliding is "the state-led debilitation or elimination of the political institutions sustaining an existing democracy." The six varieties are open-ended coups d'état, executive coups, blatant election-day vote fraud, promissory coups, executive aggrandizement, and strategic electoral manipulation and harassment. — [Journal of Democracy](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/on-democratic-backsliding/)
  - **Trend claim:** "open-ended coups d'état, executive coups, and blatant election-day vote fraud are declining while promissory coups, executive aggrandizement and strategic electoral manipulation and harassment are increasing." Bermeo argues the newer forms are "more easily reversible" and that the resulting regimes are "shorter-lived and less authoritarian." — [Journal of Democracy](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/on-democratic-backsliding/)
  - **Executive aggrandizement:** from memory, Bermeo writes that it "occurs when elected executives weaken checks on executive power one by one, undertaking a series of institutional changes that hamper the power of opposition forces to challenge executive preferences," usually through *legal* channels (referenda, legislation, courts) **[unverified; the fetched page did not show this text]**.

#### 10. Ruth Ben-Ghiat, *Strongmen: Mussolini to the Present* (2020)
- **Structure:** Part I, Getting to Power; Part II, Tools of Rule; Part III, Losing Power **[part titles unverified]**. The "tools of rule" are **"A Greater Nation"** (promise of national restoration), **propaganda**, **corruption**, **virility**, and **violence**. — [search summaries of the book; author's site](https://ruthbenghiat.com/strongmen/); [Utrecht review, "On Strongmen"](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/426327/On_Strongmen.pdf?sequence=1)
- She covers three eras: fascist (1919-1945), military coups (1950s-1990), and "new authoritarian" (1990-present). Her focus is personalist rule, in which loyalty to the leader replaces institutions. Masculinity is a "political weapon," and corruption both pays off cronies and is denounced in others. — [author's site](https://ruthbenghiat.com/strongmen/); [Greg Olear interview](https://gregolear.substack.com/p/strongman-virile-man-with-ruth-ben)
- **Limitations:** The book is a broad comparative history of the strongman *type*, not a set of necessary and sufficient criteria. Critiques were not fetched **[gap]**.

#### 11. Optional: Federico Finchelstein; Lawrence (Laurence) Britt
- **Finchelstein (*From Fascism to Populism in History*, 2017):** emphasizes "the significant differences between populism as a form of democracy and fascism as a form of dictatorship." Populism shares a history with fascism but is a distinct trajectory. Postwar populism (e.g., Peronism) is in his view a reformulation of fascism that keeps electoral democracy and drops dictatorship and extreme violence **[the last clause is my summary of his thesis, unverified in a fetched source]**. — [UC Press via Bookshare/Labyrinth descriptions](https://www.labyrinthbooks.com/from-fascism-to-populism-in-history)
- **Britt, "Fascism Anyone?" (*Free Inquiry*, 2003):** 14 characteristics: powerful and continuing nationalism; disdain for human rights; identification of enemies/scapegoats; supremacy of the military; rampant sexism; controlled mass media; obsession with national security; religion and government intertwined; corporate power protected; labor power suppressed; disdain for intellectuals and the arts; obsession with crime and punishment; rampant cronyism and corruption; fraudulent elections. — [Wikipedia, Definitions of fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism)
  - **Caveat:** published in a secular-humanist magazine, not peer-reviewed. Britt was not an academic specialist, and the list draws on a few regimes (Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, Franco, Salazar, Papadopoulos, Pinochet, Suharto) that mix fascist and military-authoritarian cases **[case list from memory, unverified]**.

### Inferences
- The two most operationally useful instruments for coding behavior are Levitsky & Ziblatt's 15 sub-questions and Bermeo's typology of backsliding mechanisms. Paxton's stages are useful for assessing *trajectory*, especially stage 2 (rooting via elite alliance) and stage 3 (elites handing over power).
- Eco's and Britt's lists are the easiest to apply and the most prone to false positives, because neither sets a threshold (Eco says one trait suffices).

## Q1: Where do the frameworks agree (core shared features) and where do they diverge?

### Takeaway
Nearly all of them converge on six features: a leader cult or personalist authority; an us/them enemy construction (internal enemies or plots); the legitimation of violence; hostility to truth, the press and intellectuals; ultranationalism with a decline-and-rebirth narrative; and the subordination of the individual or of rights to the group. They diverge on whether fascism is defined by **ideology** (Griffin, Eco, Payne), by **behavior or trajectory** (Paxton), or by **tactics** (Stanley), and on whether revolutionary ambition and mass mobilization are required.

- **Victimhood and enemies:** Paxton passion 3 (victimhood justifying "any action"); Eco 7-8 (plot obsession; enemies "too strong and too weak"); Stanley pillar 6 (victimhood); Levitsky & Ziblatt indicator 2 (rivals as "existential threat" or "foreign agents"); Britt (scapegoats). — [Gardner](https://dgardner.substack.com/p/is-it-fascism); [Eco](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/); [L&Z table](https://journals.umcs.pl/al/article/downloadSuppFile/12210/6186)
- **Violence:** Paxton passion 8 and "redemptive violence"; Payne ("positive evaluation of violence and war"); Eco 9 (permanent warfare); L&Z indicator 3; Snyder lesson 6 (paramilitaries); Ben-Ghiat (violence as a tool of rule). — sources as above; [Snyder](https://snyder.substack.com/p/on-tyranny)
- **Leader cult and masculinity:** Paxton passions 6-7; Payne ("charismatic, personal style of command"; "masculine principle"); Eco 12 (machismo); Stanley pillar 8 (sexual anxiety); Ben-Ghiat ("virility"). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism); [Ben-Ghiat site](https://ruthbenghiat.com/strongmen/)
- **Truth, language and intellectuals:** Eco 4 and 14 (Newspeak); Stanley 2-4 (propaganda, anti-intellectualism, unreality); Snyder 9-11 and 17 ("Believe in truth," "Listen for dangerous words"); Ben-Ghiat (propaganda); Britt (controlled media, disdain for intellectuals). — sources as above
- **Divergence on method:** Paxton grounds fascism in political behavior and opportunism. Mann (as summarized by Riley) and Griffin insist on a coherent ideology. Renton prefers to study activism and violence over ideas. — [Riley, NLR](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii30/articles/dylan-riley-enigmas-of-fascism); [Wikipedia, Palingenetic ultranationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism)
- **Divergence on threshold:** Eco says one trait can be enough. Griffin requires the full core myth. Paxton requires a mass party *plus* collaboration with elites *plus* the abandonment of democratic liberties. — [Eco](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism); [enotes](https://www.enotes.com/topics/anatomy-fascism)

### Inferences
- The overlap is strongest at the level of rhetoric and behavior (enemies, violence, truth). The disagreements concentrate on structural requirements: a mass party, paramilitary mobilization, revolutionary aims and expansionism. When applying the frameworks to a modern leader, these structural items will most likely separate "fascist" from "authoritarian/populist" verdicts.

### Gaps
- No published systematic cross-framework coding study was found.

## Q2: Which frameworks distinguish fascism from authoritarianism, populism or conservatism, and how?

### Takeaway
Linz, Griffin, Payne, Paxton and Finchelstein all draw explicit lines. Fascism is revolutionary, ideological and mobilizational. Authoritarianism is demobilizing and run on a "mentality." Conservatism is an elite ally and not a fascist itself. Populism is electoral and democratic in form. Stanley, Eco and Britt draw these lines least clearly.

### Cited Findings
- **Linz:** authoritarian regimes have "limited … pluralism," "mentalities" rather than "elaborate and guiding ideology," and "without extensive nor intensive political mobilization." All three contrast with totalitarian and fascist mobilization. — [Populism Studies](https://www.populismstudies.org/Vocabulary/authoritarianism/)
- **Griffin:** the palingenetic-populist-ultranationalist synthesis "differentiates fascism from para-fascism and other authoritarian, nationalist ideologies." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism)
- **Payne:** lists "anticonservatism" among the fascist *negations*, while noting fascists' willingness to ally with the right. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism)
- **Paxton:** fascism needs "uneasy but effective collaboration with traditional elites." His stages 2-3 make conservative elites the gatekeepers, and a fascist regime can "slip into traditional authoritarian rule" (entropy). Fascism and authoritarianism are therefore points on one trajectory. — [enotes](https://www.enotes.com/topics/anatomy-fascism); [stage summary](https://www.nakedcapitalism.com/2019/07/robert-o-paxtons-the-five-stages-of-fascism.html)
- **Finchelstein:** populism is "a form of democracy," fascism "a form of dictatorship." — [Labyrinth/UC Press description](https://www.labyrinthbooks.com/from-fascism-to-populism-in-history)
- **Levitsky & Way / Bermeo / Levitsky & Ziblatt:** these do not define fascism at all. They describe hybrid regimes and incremental, legalistic erosion of democracy by elected incumbents. — [MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/17196/summary); [JoD Bermeo](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/on-democratic-backsliding/)
- **Stanley** studies "fascist politics" (tactics), which may occur without a fascist regime, so he deliberately blurs the regime-type boundary. Critics see this as concept stretching. — [Libby](https://share.libbyapp.com/title/3758632); [SD Jewish World](https://www.sdjewishworld.com/?p=114480)

### Inferences
- A leader can score high on the Levitsky & Ziblatt or Bermeo behavioral indicators and still fall short of fascism under Griffin, Payne or Paxton if there is no mass paramilitary party, no revolutionary new-order project, or no expansionism. Equally, Stanley- or Eco-style "fascist politics" can be present in a competitive-authoritarian or backsliding democracy.

### Gaps
- Paxton's own writings revising his view of whether contemporary movements qualify (he shifted after January 2021) are outside scope. They are noted only as an author's own application, and I did not fetch them.

## Q3: Which criteria are observable behaviors (useful for empirical comparison) and which are ideological or structural?

### Takeaway
The behavioral, codable criteria come mostly from the democracy and authoritarianism literature (L&Z, Bermeo, Levitsky & Way, Snyder, Ben-Ghiat) and from Stanley's tactics. Griffin, Eco and much of Payne are ideological or psychological. Paxton combines emotional "passions" with structural conditions that can be observed: a mass party, elite collaboration and paramilitary violence.

### Cited Findings
- **Directly observable (speech or acts):** all 15 L&Z sub-questions (e.g., refusing to accept credible election results; calling rivals criminals or foreign agents; failing to condemn supporters' violence; threatening legal action against media). — [L&Z table](https://journals.umcs.pl/al/article/downloadSuppFile/12210/6186)
- **Observable institutional change:** Bermeo's executive aggrandizement and "strategic electoral manipulation and harassment"; Levitsky & Way's repeated violations of electoral, civil-liberty and media rules. — [JoD Bermeo](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/on-democratic-backsliding/); [MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/17196/summary)
- **Observable rhetoric:** Stanley's mythic past, unreality and victimhood; Eco's Newspeak and plot obsession; Ben-Ghiat's propaganda and virility displays. These can be measured with content analysis. — [Stanley description](https://shop.arizona.edu/9780525511854-TRADE); [Eco](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/); [Ben-Ghiat](https://ruthbenghiat.com/strongmen/)
- **Structural or organizational (observable but large-scale):** Paxton's mass party of militants and elite collaboration; Payne's militarized mass mobilization; Snyder's paramilitaries and one-party state; Linz's degree of pluralism and mobilization. — [enotes](https://www.enotes.com/topics/anatomy-fascism); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions_of_fascism); [Snyder](https://snyder.substack.com/p/on-tyranny); [Populism Studies](https://www.populismstudies.org/Vocabulary/authoritarianism/)
- **Ideological or psychological (hard to observe directly):** Griffin's palingenetic myth; Eco's cult of tradition, irrationalism and heroism/death cult; Paxton passions 1, 4 and 7; Linz's "mentality." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palingenetic_ultranationalism); [Eco](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/06/22/ur-fascism/); [Gardner](https://dgardner.substack.com/p/is-it-fascism)

### Inferences
- A practical checklist could use three tiers: (1) behavioral indicators from L&Z and Bermeo, coded yes/no with evidence; (2) rhetorical markers from Stanley, Eco and Ben-Ghiat, coded by frequency; (3) structural thresholds for fascism from Paxton, Payne and Griffin (mass militant party or paramilitaries, elite alliance, revolutionary rebirth project, expansionism, abandonment of democratic liberties), which decide whether the case counts as fascism or as authoritarianism or populism.

### Gaps
- None of the sources supply validated weights or thresholds for combining indicators, except the L&Z claim that one indicator is cause for concern **[unverified]** and Eco's claim that one feature is enough.
